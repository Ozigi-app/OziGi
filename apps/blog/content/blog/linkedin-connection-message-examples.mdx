> **TL;DR:** A LinkedIn connection note has 200 characters to do one job: give a real reason you two specifically should be connected. Seven examples below cover the common situations, same community, their content, a trigger event, a mutual, an event, a founder ask, and a straight professional reach-out. The accept is not the win, though. The win is the conversation after, so the second half covers the first DM and the light three-message sequence that turns accepts into calls without turning you into LinkedIn spam.

The connection request is the easy part. Acceptance rates for a decent note to a reasonable target run high, because accepting costs nothing. The graveyard is what happens next: either silence, or the pitch that arrives 40 seconds after the accept and gets you mentally filed under spam forever.

So this guide covers both halves. First the note that gets you in, then the messages that make being in worth anything.

## Should You Even Send a Note With the Connection Request?

Yes when you have a real reason, no when you would have to invent one. A blank request from someone with a relevant title and a clear profile often outperforms a bad note, because a bad note ("I came across your profile and was impressed") confirms you are running a script. The rule: if your note could be sent to 500 people unchanged, send nothing instead.

When you do have a reason, 200 characters is enough. Every example below fits the limit.

## 7 Connection Message Examples by Situation

**1. Same community.**

> We're both in [community name], I mostly lurk in the [channel] discussions. Your point about [specific thing] matched what I've been seeing. Figured we should be connected here too.

**2. Their content.**

> Read your post on [specific topic]. The part about [specific detail] is the first honest take I've seen on it. Would like to keep your writing in my feed.

The detail is the whole message. Naming something not in the title proves you read past it, which nobody running automation does.

**3. Trigger event.**

> Saw [company] just [shipped X, raised, hit a milestone]. I work with teams at exactly that stage on [problem area], and I'd like to follow how you approach it.

Honest about the professional interest, no pitch. The pitch this early kills the accept.

**4. The mutual.**

> [Name] mentioned you're the person to know on [topic]. No agenda beyond that, they're usually right about people.

Only if the mutual actually said it. Fabricated mutuals get checked, and one check costs the relationship on both sides.

**5. The event.**

> We were both at [event]. I didn't get to say hi during [session], but your question about [thing] was the one worth asking. Connecting so next time I do say hi.

**6. The founder ask.**

> Building [product] for [audience]. You've clearly dealt with [problem], your [post, role, project] shows it. Not selling, trying to learn from 20 people who live this. Open to connecting?

The founder version works for the same reason it works in [cold email](https://ozigi.app/blog/cold-email-templates-that-get-replies): the honesty is the tactic, and it dies the moment the "not selling" becomes a bait.

**7. The straight professional.**

> Your work on [specific project or area] overlaps with what I do at [company] more than almost anyone in my feed. Keeping my network to people whose work I actually want to see. Yours qualifies.

## How Do You Find the Right People to Connect With?

Work backwards from your [ideal customer profile](https://ozigi.app/blog/ideal-customer-profile-for-developer-products), then use the signals LinkedIn hands you: who posts about your problem space, who comments on your competitors' content, who just changed into the role that owns your problem, and who works at companies with the job postings that reveal the pain. Ten connections a day chosen this way beat a hundred scraped by title alone, and staying at a modest daily volume also keeps you clear of LinkedIn's restrictions on connection request behavior, which have tightened every year.

## What Do You Send After They Accept?

Nothing for a day or two, then a message with no ask in it. The 40-second pitch after the accept is the single most common LinkedIn outreach mistake, and it converts a warm accept into a spam report reflex.

The light sequence that works:

| Message | Timing | Content | Ask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First DM | 1 to 2 days after accept | React to something real of theirs, add one useful thought | None |
| Second DM | When they reply, or 5 to 7 days | The bridge: name the problem you both care about, share one insight or resource | Soft: "want the longer version?" |
| Third DM | Only if engaged | The honest ask, small | 15 minutes, or the demo link |

**First DM example:**

> Your point about [topic] stuck with me, especially [detail]. We hit the same wall last quarter and solved it differently: [one sentence]. Curious whether you tried that route.

**The bridge example:**

> That thread about [problem] keeps coming up with people I talk to. I put together [a short teardown, a checklist, a comparison] on it, want me to send it over?

The sequence logic mirrors [the B2B email arc](https://ozigi.app/blog/b2b-lead-generation-email-templates): relevance, then value, then the ask, and it stops the moment silence says stop. Three unanswered DMs is the ceiling. Past that you are the person people screenshot.

Tone matters as much as structure here, arguably more than in email, because DMs sit next to messages from actual friends. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**What should I say in a LinkedIn connection request?**
One real reason you two specifically should be connected, in under 200 characters: a shared community, something specific from their content, a trigger event, or a genuine mutual. If you cannot name a specific reason, send the request without a note rather than with a generic one.

**Is it better to send a LinkedIn connection request with or without a message?**
With, when the message contains a real specific reason. Without, when the alternative is a template line like "I came across your profile." A blank request lets your profile do the talking; a generic note announces automation.

**How long should a LinkedIn connection message be?**
Under the 200-character limit, and the limit is a feature. One reason, one specific detail, no pitch. Everything longer belongs in the conversation after the accept, not the request.

**What do you send after someone accepts your connection request?**
Wait a day or two, then a no-ask message reacting to something real of theirs. Pitching immediately after the accept is the fastest way to get archived. Build one exchange first, offer value second, and make the small ask third, only if they have engaged.

**How many LinkedIn connection requests can I send per day?**
Keep it modest, on the order of 10 to 20 well-chosen requests a day, well under LinkedIn's weekly invitation limits. Volume is not the constraint that matters anyway: acceptance and reply rates track how well-chosen the person is, not how many you sent.
