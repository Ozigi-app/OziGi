## TL;DR

Automate everything that happens before a draft exists: ingestion, extraction, structure, and style enforcement. Put one human approval gate between the draft and the public. At that gate, a person adds the facts only they know, checks every claim, and decides whether it goes out. That costs about five to ten minutes per post. Skipping it saves those minutes and risks wrong facts, tone-deaf timing, and a feed that reads like everyone else's. Below is where the gate goes, what to check, and a ten-point checklist.

The loudest pitch in AI content tooling is one click from topic to published post. No human in the middle.

I understand why it sells. Review is the slow part. But the thing that makes a post worth reading is also the thing a model cannot get on its own: the bug you fixed on Thursday night, the number from your dashboard, the opinion you hold that your audience already knows you hold.

So the question is not whether to keep a human in the loop. It is where to put them so the process stays fast.

## Why does AI-generated content need human review?

Because a model drafts from the input it was given, and it has no way to check that input against reality or against your audience's context. It will produce a confident sentence from a wrong number as readily as from a right one.

Four failure modes show up in practice:

1. **Wrong facts.** A figure gets rounded, a name gets misspelled, a timeline gets compressed. The sentence reads fine, which is the problem.
2. **Missing context.** Your source notes are a snapshot. If you shipped a hotfix after writing them, the draft announces a bug you already fixed.
3. **Tone-deaf timing.** The model does not know your industry had an outage this morning or that a competitor just launched. A cheerful post on the wrong day costs you.
4. **Sameness.** Without anything specific added, generated posts converge. Volume goes up and recognition goes down.

None of these show up in a quality metric until they are public.

## Which parts of the pipeline should you automate?

Automate everything where the output is structural and checkable. Review everything where the output makes a claim on your behalf.

| Stage | Automate? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ingesting source material (links, files, notes) | Yes | Mechanical. No judgment involved. |
| Extracting the main points | Yes | A model does this well, and you can verify it fast. |
| Choosing the angle for each post | Partly | Suggest automatically, decide yourself. The angle is your positioning. |
| Formatting for each platform | Yes | Length limits and structure are rules. |
| Style and vocabulary enforcement | Yes | A banned-word list and cadence checks run in code without a person. |
| Factual claims and numbers | Review | Wrong figures damage trust. |
| Opinions and positions | Review | These speak for you. |
| The decision to publish | Human only | This is the part with consequences. |
| Scheduling a post you already approved | Yes | Timing is mechanical once the content is settled. |

The pattern: let machines do what has a right answer, let people do what has a consequence.

## Where should the approval gate go?

Between generation and publishing. Not before generation, where you are reviewing nothing, and not after, where mistakes are already public.

## What should you check in a review?

Check the things a model cannot know, then the things it commonly gets wrong. Five to ten minutes covers it if you follow a fixed order.

**The ten-point review checklist**

1. Every number has a source you can find. If you cannot find it, cut it.
2. Names, companies, product names, and handles are spelled correctly.
3. The post contains at least one detail only you could have supplied: a real figure, a date, a decision, a failure.
4. Any claim about a competitor or another person is something you can defend in public.
5. The post does not announce something that has changed since your source notes.
6. Nothing about today's news makes the post look tone-deaf.
7. Links work and point where the text says they do.
8. You would say this sentence out loud to a colleague. Read it aloud once.
9. No AI tells survived: run the text through the [free slop checker](https://ozigi.app/slop-checker) if you are unsure.
10. The call to action matches what you want, and there is only one.

Items 1 to 3 catch most real problems. If you only have two minutes, run those three.

## How is this different from fully automated content tools?

Fully automated tools optimize for throughput. The measure of success is posts published per week, and the review step is a cost to remove. A human-in-the-loop tool optimizes for trust, with your audience and with your own output.

The tradeoff is real and worth stating plainly. Full automation wins on raw volume. If your goal is to flood a feed with posts, a gate slows you down.

For a founder, an engineer, or a small team, the goal is almost never raw volume. It is being recognized by a specific audience as someone worth following. That depends on specifics and judgment, which are the two things the gate protects.

There is also a platform angle. Networks tighten rules on repetitive, automated behavior over time, and accounts that look automated are the first ones to feel it. A visible human decision on every post is a safer posture than a pipeline that publishes unattended.

## How do you keep review from becoming the bottleneck?

Make review fast by making drafts good, and make drafts good by fixing the input once.

- **Fix repeated edits at the source.** If you delete the same sentence every week, add a rule to your persona's style guide so the draft stops producing it. The [brand voice guide](https://ozigi.app/blog/how-to-build-a-brand-voice-ai-can-follow) covers how.
- **Batch your review.** Approve a week of posts in one sitting instead of context-switching ten times.
- **Review in a fixed order.** The checklist above, same order every time, becomes a two-minute habit.
- **Approve, then schedule.** Once a post passes, let a scheduler handle the timing. Only content you have approved should ever be queued.
- **Do not review fatigued.** A draft you glance at while distracted is closer to no review than to a quick one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does AI-generated content need a human to review it?**
Yes, if it speaks for you or your company. A model cannot verify the facts it writes, and it does not know what happened in your business since you wrote your source notes. A person needs to check claims, add specifics, and decide to publish. Review takes five to ten minutes per post.

**What parts of content creation should be automated?**
Ingesting source material, extracting main points, formatting for each platform, enforcing style rules and banned vocabulary, and scheduling posts you have already approved. These tasks have checkable answers. Factual claims, opinions, and the decision to publish should stay with a person.

**Where should the human approval step go in an AI content workflow?**
Between draft generation and publishing. Reviewing before generation has nothing to check, and reviewing after publication is too late. In Ozigi, drafts stay staged until you edit and publish them yourself.

**What is the 90/10 rule for AI content?**
The engine handles about 90% of the work: ingestion, extraction, structure, and style enforcement. The author handles the last 10%: real numbers, context, opinions, and the details only they know. That 10% decides whether anyone remembers the post.

**What should I check before publishing an AI-written post?**
Verify every number against its source, check names and spellings, confirm at least one detail only you could supply, make sure nothing has changed since your source notes, and read the post aloud once. A ten-point checklist covers the rest.

**Is fully automated AI content ever a good idea?**
It can work for low-stakes, high-volume material where an error costs little, such as internal summaries. For anything under your name in public, the risk of wrong facts and generic output outweighs the time saved.
